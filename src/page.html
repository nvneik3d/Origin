How this world works

A cell

  • Swims and turns. Bodies push apart, so nothing overlaps.
  • Tastes food where it is and remembers if it was better a moment ago.
  • Leaks waste that poisons whoever sits in it.
  • Has a brain of 1 to 16 neurons. Brain size is inherited and every neuron costs energy.

Bodies

  • Each cell decides how sticky to be. Stickiness changes slowly, like real glue proteins being made or broken down.
  • Two touching family members who both want to stick form a lasting attachment, a spring that holds until one lets go or it's pulled too far. Each cell can hold up to six.
  • A sticky cell's daughter stays attached, so bodies also grow by dividing.
  • Family close by lowers a cell's running cost (shared digestion), counting up to six neighbours.
  • Cells inside a body are hard to eat and hit back harder. They can pass a signal to each other.

Hunters

  • The hollow rings. They can't eat food, only grazers.
  • A loose grazer is easy prey. One held on all sides usually gets away.
  • Grazers can strike back, harder the more cells they hold on to.
  • Since bodies got bigger, hunters rarely last long. When they die out, a few newcomers drift in.

The world

  • Food patches drift. Tap the jelly or press feed to add more.
  • Seasons: food grows back fast in summer, barely at all in winter.
  • Large and Huge worlds run on your graphics card, where every cell moves at the same instant. They still form fewer, smaller bodies than Small (in early tests about 25–45% of cells in bodies, against about 90%). We ruled out the springs, the pushes, grid limits and double-counted bites. The cause is still open, so compare worlds of the same size.

Designed by us the world, the senses, the possible actions, the costs.

Left to evolution every brain weight, brain size, and whether to stick, bite, share, call or remember.

1 · Bodies were torn apart

In the old rules, 84–97% of broken holds were cells pulled apart while both still wanted to stick. A hold lasted only 4 to 6 steps. Now touching family form lasting attachments, and stickiness changes slowly. An attachment holds about 60 steps on average.

2 · Big clumps starve in the middle

About 95% of deaths were starvation and under 1% were hunters. A body only feeds through its edge, but every cell inside it costs energy. Real organisms hit the same surface-against-volume problem. More food just made more cells, and sharing food along attachments didn't help.

3 · One hand-made rule holds bodies together

Switch off the family bonus (family close by lowers a cell's running cost) and bodies vanish. It used to stop counting at three neighbours, so a cell deep inside a body gained nothing over one at the edge. Letting it count up to six, slightly weaker per neighbour, makes bodies about five times bigger than before.

4 · Predators make small groups

With the bonus off, three times more hunters, and hunters that struggle to tear cells out of bodies, 10–27% of cells lived in groups of about three (3–9% without). Real algae did something similar: they evolved 8-cell colonies within about 100 generations of meeting a predator that could only swallow single cells (Boraas, Seale & Boxhorn, 1998).

A side effect: with nine in ten grazers held safely inside bodies, hunters now mostly starve. They die out roughly every 60 steps and a few newcomers drift in each time, so hunter brains never get the chance to evolve. We tried weaker stings, weaker shielding, more food per kill, no fighting back and cheaper upkeep for hunters. None of them reliably brought hunters back (two worlds each, 6,000 steps).

The honest summary: body size in this dish is mostly set by one rule we wrote, how much family neighbours lower a cell's costs. We picked a middle setting. Make it 50% stronger and bodies average 38 cells, but only because cells deep inside then live almost for free, which isn't how real bodies work. Predators and food on their own didn't build big organisms here, at least not within 6,000 steps.

30,000 steps later

The same two test worlds with today's rules, left running headless for 30,000 steps. In one, bodies kept growing: the average doubled from about 20 to about 40 cells, and the largest passed 300. In the other, they swung between 16 and 33 cells. In both, brains shrank, from 6 neurons to under 2 in one world and about 4 in the other. Every neuron costs energy, and a cell held inside a body seems to need fewer of them. Two worlds is a hint, not proof. (The earlier version, before lasting attachments, flattened out after a few thousand steps and stayed flat for 100,000.)

Hunters survive on their own in every world, and grazers and hunters rise and fall in cycles, the classic predator–prey pattern (watch the chart in the Live panel). But within 10,000 steps hunters don't push grazers into bigger bodies or bigger brains. Grazer brains actually end up a little smaller when hunted. No arms race yet.

Left for 60,000 steps (two worlds), the hunters got much better at hunting: kills per step rose by about 65% (from 3.5 to 5.8 and from 5.1 to 8.5), and hunters came to outnumber grazers about two to one. Grazers didn't find an answer. Their bodies stayed small and both sides' brains shrank to save energy. So far the hunters are winning by getting cheaper and more efficient, not smarter.

At first, fighting back swings things to the grazers. They often wipe the hunters out, then relax into smaller bodies because the danger is gone.

Long run: two worlds left for 40,000 steps tell a different story. By about step 8,000 the hunters adapt and stop dying out, and their brains grow and stay bigger.

When prey can hit back, hunting takes more brain: hunters kept about 7 neurons instead of shrinking to 4 or 5. That is the first sign of an arms race in this dish. It's two worlds only, so treat it as a hint, not proof.

What each rule actually does earlier version

Each rule was switched off in the same four starting worlds (seeds 11, 22, 33, 44), run headless for 6,000 steps and averaged over steps 2,000–6,000. If a rule matters, switching it off should change the world.

Holding on is the one rule evolution clearly uses: nearly every creature ends up sticky, and worlds where it works hold 43% more life. Daughters that stay attached don't make bodies bigger, because loose cells re-attach anyway. Cells don't seem to use the signals inside a body yet. In an earlier version, memory showed no clear effect either. Four seeds is a small sample; differences under about 5% can appear by chance.

Dig deeper

origin stands on decades of work in cellular automata, artificial life and real biology. These are the projects and studies behind its ideas, and good places to start.

Play with it

  • Conway's Game of LifeThe 1970 classic. Four rules on a grid make gliders, guns and patterns that copy themselves.
  • Particle Life, Koen van GilstColoured dots that only pull or push form cell-like blobs. Runs in your browser.
  • Clusters, Jeffrey VentrellaThe particle-life idea that much of this family of projects grew from.
  • The BibitesCreatures with small brains that eat, breed and evolve. Free download.
  • ALIENA GPU artificial life world of particle creatures that evolve.

Cellular automata

  • Lenia, Bert ChanA smooth Game of Life with hundreds of lifelike species.

  • A New Kind of Science, Stephen WolframA big book on how simple rules make complex things. Free to read.
  • LifeWikiThousands of Game of Life patterns, catalogued.
